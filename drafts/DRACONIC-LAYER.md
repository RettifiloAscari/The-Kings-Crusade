# Design Draft — The Draconic Layer

**Status: PROPOSAL. NOT CANON. Nothing here is decided.**

Scope: where dragons and wyverns sit across the whole campaign, beyond what is already
locked. Two things are canon already and are not reopened here — see §0. Everything past
that is new invention needing sign-off, per the usual cycle in `CLAUDE.md`.

This draft exists because three canon facts now point at the same unwritten layer and
none of them can be finished without it: Harrowmark's wyverns are beasts, Xavier earns
"the Wyvernheart" from a wyvern's back mid-campaign, and dragons are meant to play a large
role in the campaign generally. The third one is the only part actually undecided.

---

## 0. What is already locked — not reopened here

- **Harrowmark's wyverns are beasts, not magic.** Hunted in the crags with pikes and
  ropes, an ordinary and appalling hazard of a mundane land. This is why Harrowmark can
  have wyverns and still be unmagical in the land — see the distinction in `CLAUDE.md`
  between worked magic (ordinary, portable, belongs to people) and resident magic
  (belongs to Elduvaine alone).
- **Xavier earns "the Wyvernheart" during the crusade**, airborne, in a battle going
  badly. Not before it. No document may use the name in narration set earlier.
- **Ruleset is 2014 (SRD 5.1).** Any stat block this draft's options lead to is built
  against that dataset, at `src/2014/en/`.

## 1. What kind of thing is a dragon in this setting?

The load-bearing question. Everything else in this draft is downstream of it, because
the answer decides whether dragons are a Harrowmark thing, an Elduvaine thing, or both —
and those are very different campaigns.

**Option A — Dragons are Elduvaine's, and they are resident magic given a body.**
Wyverns are what a mundane land produces: big, dangerous, unmagical animals. Dragons are
what a *magical* land produces at the same evolutionary slot — not beasts that happen to
know magic, but something closer to the Willing Road or the Listening Water taking on
shape and appetite. A dragon in Elduvaine is a habit with teeth.
*Buys:* the cleanest possible thematic contrast with Harrowmark, and ties dragons directly
to the drainage — a dragon should visibly suffer, change, or grow strange as its home
region is drained, the same way a wood does in the Held Winter. Gives Vale a reason to
want them controlled or removed rather than merely fought.
*Costs:* the most conceptual work. "Resident magic given a body" needs careful handling so
it does not collapse into "a wizard's pet" or "a random monster."

**Option B — Dragons are old, and older than the wards.** They predate Elduvaine's
current magical settlement, sleeping in deep places (mountains, the sea floor, under the
Archive itself) since before the habits were established. They are not resident magic;
they are what the land had before it became resident, and the Ysolde Archive's deepest
vaults may be built on, in, or around one.
*Buys:* a direct hook into the *What the deepest vaults actually contain* open item,
and a genuinely frightening set piece — waking something that predates the kingdom.
*Costs:* risks answering the vaults question by implication before you are ready to
answer it outright. Usable only with discipline about what gets said aloud.

**Option C — Dragons are foreign, and Vale brought them.** Not native to either realm;
Vale acquired, bound, or bred them as part of his war machine, alongside or instead of a
conventional army. Elduvaine's own draconic history, if it has one, is separate and
untouched by this.
*Buys:* the simplest to place and the most game-forward — every dragon the party meets is
an enemy asset with a straightforward reason to be there.
*Costs:* the weakest fit with "dragons play a large role in the campaign" as a wonder
element rather than a threat roster entry, and it wastes the contrast with Harrowmark's
wyverns, which is the single best piece of texture this layer could have.

**Recommended: A, with B as a single deep-vault secret rather than the general rule.**
Elduvaine's dragons are resident magic given shape and appetite — visibly tied to the
land's habits, and visibly harmed by the drainage — and one very old exception sleeps
somewhere near or under the Archive, answering to neither Vale nor the party until the
campaign needs it to. This keeps the general population usable throughout without
spending the vaults' answer early.

## 2. Vale's army — does he have dragons, and if so, how?

Depends on §1. If dragons are resident magic given a body (A), Vale cannot simply recruit
one the way he recruited soldiers — a dragon that serves him is either coerced, or it is
being drained the same way the land is.

**Option A — He has none, and that is the point.** Vale's army is human and mundane by
design; dragons in Elduvaine are wild, native, and hostile to him specifically, because
draining the land hurts them and they know exactly who is doing it. This makes dragons a
second front the party can potentially turn into an ally against Vale rather than another
monster to clear.
*Buys:* the clearest use of "dragons play a large role" as wonder and possible alliance
rather than escalating threat tier; gives the party a non-combat path with the setting's
most dangerous creatures.
*Costs:* removes dragons from Vale's direct threat roster, so the campaign's mechanical
escalation has to come from somewhere else (Vale's own casting, his officers, constructs
built from what he has read).

**Option B — He has bound one or a few, at terrible cost.** Somewhere in the deepest
vaults he found a way to compel resident-magic dragons the way he compelled the wards —
by rule rather than by force, exploiting the same kind of loophole that let him open
Elduvaine in the first place. A bound dragon in his service is visibly wrong: pained,
diminished, working for him the way a hostage works for a captor.
*Buys:* an extraordinary set piece (freeing a bound dragon, or being forced to fight one
that does not want to fight), and it stays perfectly in character for Vale — he does not
tame, he finds the rule that makes refusal impossible.
*Costs:* needs careful writing so it reads as horror rather than as "boss has a dragon
mount," which is the generic version of this idea and undersells what makes Vale specific.

**Recommended: B, singular — one bound dragon, used once, at a moment the campaign needs
its worst version of Vale on display.** Not a mount he casually commands and not a combat
tier upgrade; a single scene where the party sees exactly what "he finds the rule that
makes refusal impossible" costs someone who is not human. Everywhere else, dragons are
wild, native, and not his — which keeps Option A's alliance potential fully available.

## 3. The battle where Xavier becomes the Wyvernheart

Canon already fixes the shape: airborne, on a wyvern, in a battle going badly, during the
crusade. What is open is *which* battle, in *which* module — the Third Crusade's own arc
gives four candidates already named in `CLAUDE.md`: the march, the siege of Vindana, the
field battle that proves the enemy beatable, and the approach to the decision at the
gates.

**Option A — The field battle (Arsuf-keyed).** This is the campaign's proof-of-concept
battle, the one that shows Vale's army can lose. Xavier taking to a wyvern mid-battle,
against a going-badly moment, and turning it, is the single highest-leverage place to put
a title-earning scene: the battle that proves the war is winnable is also the one where
its king becomes legendary.
*Buys:* maximum narrative efficiency — one scene does double duty as the campaign's
turning point and its title-earning moment.
*Costs:* stacks two huge beats on one battle, which risks crowding the module.

**Option B — The siege of Vindana.** The campaign's central, most sustained set piece.
A siege gives more room for "going badly" to develop over multiple scenes before the
airborne turn, and a wyvern breaking a siege line has real tactical drama a field battle
doesn't offer in the same way.
*Buys:* the longest lead-up, so the moment can be properly telegraphed within the module
without foreshadowing the name itself; sieges are also where Harrowmark's wyvern-hunters
would plausibly be present in numbers, giving Xavier something to have learned from.
*Costs:* Vindana is already doing enormous structural work as the campaign's central
siege; a title-earning moment here needs to not overshadow the siege's own stakes.

**Option C — The approach, late and unexpected.** Save it for near the end, when the
party (and the table) have stopped watching for it. A late title-earning scene reads as
a genuine surprise rather than an anticipated beat.
*Buys:* the strongest surprise value, closest in spirit to how a table would react to a
name changing without warning.
*Costs:* less time afterward for the name to actually mean anything at the table before
the campaign ends — Richard carried Lionheart for years after Arsuf; a King's Crusade
Xavier who earns his name in the last module barely gets to be the Wyvernheart at all.

**Recommended: B — Vindana.** It is already the campaign's centre of gravity, it gives
Harrowmark's wyvern-hunting culture a reason to be materially present at the war's biggest
set piece, and it leaves two to three modules afterward for the name to actually be used,
referenced, and earned again in play — which a late-campaign version of this cannot do.

## 4. Do wyverns appear outside Harrowmark?

A smaller, cheaper question, but it affects whether Option B in §3 needs Harrowmark
wyverns physically present at Vindana or flown in narratively.

**Option A — Harrowmark riders travel with the army.** A cadre of Harrowmark
wyvern-hunters, repurposed as scouts or aerial skirmishers, marches with Xavier. Ordinary
equipment to them; exotic to everyone else in the coalition.
*Buys:* a built-in reason for a wyvern to be at hand exactly when Xavier needs one, and a
recurring unit the party can know by name.
*Costs:* slightly domesticates the wyverns, which are canon specifically as an appalling,
barely-managed hazard rather than a cavalry arm.

**Option B — A single wyvern, wild-caught en route.** No standing unit; the moment is
earned rather than equipped, because nobody planned for a king to end up on a wild
animal's back mid-battle.
*Buys:* keeps wyverns dangerous and untamed everywhere except in Harrowmark's own
hard-won hunting tradition; makes the title-earning scene riskier and stranger.
*Costs:* needs a specific, credible reason for a wild wyvern to be anywhere near Vindana,
which is Elduvish territory, not wyvern country.

**Recommended: A, kept small.** A handful of Harrowmark riders — not cavalry, more like
the scouts and skirmishers a hard county would actually send, present precisely because
nobody else in the coalition has this skill. Keeps wyverns dangerous (losses are canon)
while giving §3 a mechanism that does not require a wild animal to wander into a siege.

## 5. What this does not cover

Deliberately out of scope: stat blocks for any dragon or wyvern (that is a mechanical
validation pass against the SRD, after this draft is signed off); the vaults' actual
contents (§1's Option B touches this and is written to avoid answering it); and any
specific dragon's name, personality, or territory, which belongs in region-level
worldbuilding once there is a gazetteer.

## 6. Natural extension vs. new invention

**Natural extension of locked canon:** the contrast between mundane Harrowmark wyverns
and something categorically different in Elduvaine follows directly from the resident/
worked magic distinction already locked. Using Vindana as a major set piece extends
canon already naming it the central siege.

**Genuine new invention requiring sign-off:** every option in §1–§4, and in particular
whether dragons exist as a species-level fact of Elduvaine (§1), whether Vale controls
any (§2), and which module carries the title-earning battle (§3).
