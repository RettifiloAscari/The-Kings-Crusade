# Design Draft — Setting Foundation

**Status: PROPOSAL. NOT CANON. Nothing here is decided.**

This draft works the *Not yet decided* table in `CLAUDE.md` into concrete, choosable
options. It is the first of the sign-off cycle described there: proposal, then redline,
then one consolidated pass into canon. Until it is signed off and renamed
`SETTING-FOUNDATION.RESOLVED.md`, no generator may reference anything in it.

**Everything below §1 is new invention.** There is no approved canon for it to extend.
That is stated once here rather than repeated per item, because right now the split
between *natural extension* and *genuine invention* is the whole document on one side of
the line. §12 records it formally.

---

## 0. How to use this

Each item offers two to four concrete options with what they buy and what they cost, and
names a recommendation. You can pick an option, mix them, redline one, or reject the whole
item and give me the answer directly — the last is often fastest, and this draft exists so
you have something specific to react to rather than a blank page.

Sign-off marks are in §13. Mark each item **APPROVED**, **APPROVED WITH CHANGES**
(and say what), or **VETO** (and, ideally, what instead).

Items are ordered by how much else depends on them. §2, §3 and §9 are load-bearing:
almost everything downstream changes if those change. §7 and §8 can safely be deferred.

---

## 1. What is already locked

From your bootstrap answers, recorded in `CLAUDE.md` and not reopened here:

- A friendly magical kingdom, taken by an evil wizard and their army. Ruling family
  captured. Lands laid waste, people in peril.
- A king calls a crusade. The party is sent to reclaim and deliver those lands.
- Third Crusade as **structural** spine; high fantasy wins where the two disagree.
- Tone: *a strange and marvellous kingdom worth saving, and a long dangerous road to
  reach it.* Exploration and set pieces over politics. The war is the frame.
- The villain is a villain. No moral ambiguity about whether to stop the wizard.
- 4–6 players, 5th level, five hours, milestone.

**One design note on the spine, which shapes every option below.** The historical Saladin
was competent, honourable and legitimate — a genuinely admirable adversary. Keying the
wizard to him *morally* would fight your "clean villain" decision. So every option here
keys to Saladin **structurally only**: a single unified adversary who took the precious
place, holds it competently, and cannot be dislodged by anything less than a coalition.
The competence transfers. The honour does not.

## 2. The Kingdom — what makes it worth saving

The tone answer puts wonder and exploration first, so this choice matters more than any
other: it decides what the party is actually walking through.

**Option A — The Living Realm.** The land itself is enchanted. Roads that shorten for
travellers who mean well, rivers that remember what was said on their banks, forests that
keep the season they were planted in. The wizard is draining it, and the drainage is
visible: a dead mile, a river that has forgotten, a wood stuck in a winter that will not
end.
*Buys:* the strongest exploration texture of the three — every hex is a set piece, and the
damage is legible on sight, which does the emotional work without a single speech.
*Costs:* the most invention up front; the rules of the land's magic need to be consistent
enough to plan around.

**Option B — The Realm of Makers.** The world's workshop: enchanted goods, great forges,
guild-halls of artificers. Its work is in every other kingdom's armoury, which is *why*
the coalition cares. The wizard now holds the means of production and is arming with it.
*Buys:* clean logic for loot, for why allies join, and for escalating enemy equipment.
*Costs:* leans mechanical rather than marvellous; risks reading as a supply-chain war.

**Option C — The Archive.** A realm of libraries, observatories and deep vaults holding
the world's magical knowledge. The wizard did not come for land. He came for what is
shelved here, and he is reading his way toward something.
*Buys:* the strongest villain motive of the three, and a ticking clock that is not
arbitrary — he gets stronger the longer he reads.
*Costs:* libraries are static; needs deliberate work to stay adventure-forward.

**Recommended: A, with C as a single region inside it** — the Living Realm for the road
and the wonder, one great archive-city as the thing the wizard is really digging for.
That gives you legible ruin to travel through *and* a clock, without choosing between them.

## 3. The Wizard

**Option A — The Usurper-Scholar.** He was the kingdom's own court magus. He built or
maintained its wards, and he opened them from the inside. Nobody outside could have taken
this realm; that is the point.
*Buys:* explains a fast fall without making the kingdom pathetic; every ward the party
meets was designed by the man they are hunting; survivors knew him.
*Costs:* the "trusted advisor betrays" beat is familiar. Needs a specific, strange motive
to lift it.

**Option B — The Outsider.** He came from somewhere else, with an army that is not from
here. Unknowable, and the wrongness of his forces is a horror engine.
*Buys:* maximum strangeness; the army itself is a bestiary.
*Costs:* an unknowable villain is hard to hate specifically, and gives the party little
to *learn* about — which weakens investigation as a play mode.

**Option C — The Returned.** An archmage of this realm's own deep past, come back with a
claim to it, insisting he is restoring rather than conquering.
*Buys:* a villain who talks, and unsettling scenes where his claim is technically sound.
*Costs:* pulls toward the moral ambiguity you decided against. Workable only if his claim
is legally real and morally worthless.

**Recommended: A.** It gives the party a villain they can *investigate* — every locked
door in the kingdom was locked by him — and it keeps the cause clean. Pair it with a motive
from §2C: he was the archive's keeper, and he wanted to finish reading.

**Open sub-questions if A is approved:** his name; whether he is still human; whether the
ruling family knows what he is; and what he is actually building toward, which the
sourcebook must state plainly in *What Is Actually Happening (DM Only)* even though the
players may never learn it.

## 4. The King and the Call

The title says whose crusade this is. The question is how present he is.

**Option A — The King in the Field** (Richard-keyed). He leads in person, brilliantly, and
the party are his chosen champions, sent ahead where the army cannot go. His own realm
decays behind him and he does not care.
*Buys:* a vivid, quotable NPC and a clear chain of command; the party's 5th-level standing
makes sense as *his* handpicked spear-point.
*Costs:* a king who wins battles personally can upstage the party. Needs discipline about
keeping him elsewhere at the decisive moment.

**Option B — The King Who Sent Us** (Philip-keyed). He called the crusade, financed it,
and turned back early for reasons of his own. The party carries a commission from a man
who has already gone home.
*Buys:* the party owns the campaign outright; a low-grade political ache running under it.
*Costs:* removes a strong NPC from play, and makes the crusade's legitimacy wobble — which
is politics, and you asked for the war to be the frame, not the subject.

**Option C — The King Who Never Arrived** (Barbarossa-keyed). The greatest of the kings
marching drowns crossing a river in the second month. His army dissolves on the road. The
party may witness it.
*Buys:* the single best set piece in the historical record, free, and an early lesson that
the road kills.
*Costs:* works as an *element*, not as the whole answer — someone still has to be running
this crusade afterwards.

**Recommended: A as the frame, with C as an event inside it.** The party's king leads;
a second, greater king marching by another road never arrives. That is historically true,
structurally excellent, and gives the coalition its first crisis.

## 5. The Captured Ruling Family

**Option A — All held, in one place.** A single rescue objective. Clean, and the endgame
writes itself.
*Costs:* one basket; reduces the family to a quest item.

**Option B — Scattered and separately held.** Each held somewhere different, under
different conditions. Rescues become a through-line across modules.
*Buys:* repeatable structure, and lets each rescue characterise a different region.
*Costs:* risks becoming a checklist.

**Option C — One escaped.** The rest are held; one is loose in the occupied kingdom,
running what resistance survives, and does not want to be evacuated.
*Buys:* a player-facing ally with agency, a reason for the party to have local support,
and a genuine dilemma at the end.
*Costs:* an NPC with a claim on the plot needs careful handling so they do not lead it.

**Recommended: C combined with B.** One at large as the resistance contact, the rest held
in two or three places. Guy of Lusignan and Conrad of Montferrat are the useful keying
here — two figures with competing claims and no love for each other — if you want the
recovered family to have internal friction. **Flagging that as its own question:** it adds
politics, which the tone answer de-prioritised. Say the word and I keep them united.

## 6. Geography and the Road

The tone answer makes the road half the campaign, so this is not just a map question.

**Option A — The Sea Road.** Ship out, with island landfalls, a storm, and a side-conquest
that pays for the war (Richard took Cyprus almost by accident and funded the crusade with
it).
*Buys:* fast, episodic, naturally varied; the side-conquest is a superb module.
*Costs:* less overland wilderness, which is where a lot of D&D lives.

**Option B — The Long March.** Overland, through mountains, through realms that are
nominally friendly and practically not. This is the road that killed Barbarossa's army.
*Buys:* maximum peril-of-the-road, hex-crawl texture, and attrition as a real pressure.
*Costs:* slower to the actual crusade; risks the campaign becoming the journey.

**Option C — The Coalition Splits.** Both, by different armies. The party chooses a road,
and later meets what became of the other.
*Buys:* player agency over the campaign's shape, and makes §4C land hard — the army that
never arrived took the other road.
*Costs:* the most preparation; you write road content the table may not see. Mitigated by
making the unchosen road something they *encounter the aftermath of* rather than a parallel
branch to be written in full.

**Recommended: C**, written asymmetrically — the chosen road in full, the other road as
consequences and survivors met later. That is the Branch Ledger's first real entry.

## 7. The Coalition — *deferrable*

How many powers march, and how much friction. Options run from **one ally** (simple,
keeps focus on the road) through **three quarrelling powers** (Richard/Philip/Barbarossa
shape, with the Nizari as an unaligned fourth party answering to nobody).

**Recommended: two allied powers plus one unaligned third party** who will deal with
either side — the Assassins keying, which is a gift for a party that likes leverage. This
can wait until §2–§6 are settled; it changes no other item.

## 8. Magic, Gods and Cosmology — *deferrable*

Only one question needs an early answer, because §2 depends on it: **is the kingdom's
magic different in kind from everyone else's, or only in quantity?** Different in kind
gives you a distinctive realm and a reason the wizard wanted this one specifically.
The pantheon, planes and traditions can all wait for the sourcebook draft.

**Recommended: different in kind**, and the difference is whatever §2 lands on.

## 9. Timeline — how long has the occupation lasted?

This one quietly sets the emotional register, so it belongs above the deferrable items.

| Option | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Months** | Refugees still on the roads, the wound fresh, rescue plainly possible. Highest urgency, least strangeness — the kingdom is still recognisably itself. |
| **Three years** *(recommended)* | Long enough that the occupation has a bureaucracy, collaborators, and children who have known nothing else; short enough that everyone remembers before. Both registers available. |
| **A generation** | The kingdom is a story its own people half-believe. Maximum strangeness, and the liberation becomes genuinely complicated — which cuts against the clean-cause decision. |

## 10. Arc Structure

**Option A — Three acts.** The Road, The Siege, The Reclamation. Roughly 4 / 5 / 4
sessions.

**Option B — The Third Crusade's own shape.** The march; the long siege of the great port
(Acre); the field battle that proves the enemy is beatable (Arsuf); the approach; the
decision at the gates.
*Buys:* proven pacing, with the siege as the campaign's spine.

**Option C — Option B, with the historical ending on the table.** Richard came within
sight of Jerusalem twice and turned back both times, because he could take it and could
not hold it. Offering the party that same choice — with it genuinely available, not as a
trick — is the most interesting ending in the source material.

**Recommended: B as the structure, with C offered as one branch of the ending** and a
clean victory as another. **Flagging C explicitly:** it is the one element here that could
undercut a heroic finish, and it is your call whether the campaign is allowed to end that
way. It costs nothing to leave in the Branch Ledger and decide at the table.

## 11. What this does not cover

Deliberately out of scope until the above is settled: NPC rosters, the map at region
granularity, the pantheon, session-level content, stat blocks, and the Player Guide's
shape. Each gets its own draft.

## 12. Natural extension vs. new invention

**Natural extension of locked canon:** nothing. There is no approved setting canon yet.

**Genuine new invention requiring sign-off:** every named option in §2–§10.

**Constrained by locked canon** (so not free invention, but not extension either): that
the kingdom is friendly and magical; that the wizard is evil and has an army; that the
family is captured; that a king calls the crusade; that the party is sent. Those are
premise, and no option above contradicts them.
